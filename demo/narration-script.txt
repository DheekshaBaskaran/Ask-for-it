Women in the United States are still paid less than men for the same work. Part of that gap starts at the offer table. Research shows women are less likely to negotiate, and are often judged more harshly than men when they do. So many accept the first number, and that small gap grows with every raise, for an entire career.
That's why I built Ask For It. Most advice stops at: just negotiate. But knowing you should ask, and actually feeling ready to ask, are two very different things. Ask For It lets you practice, so asking feels normal before the real conversation. It works in four steps: know your worth, find your words, rehearse, and walk in ready.
Building for this space came with real constraints. First, salary details are sensitive. Your numbers stay in your browser, nothing is stored on a server, and the A.I. key never reaches the page. Second, advice for women has to account for backlash. Simply telling women to be more aggressive can backfire, so the app coaches you to be warm and firm, using relationship-minded scripts based on research by Hannah Riley Bowles. Third, A.I. must never invent facts in a salary conversation. The calculator only uses your own numbers, and the wording helper is told never to make up results. Fourth, many people don't know their market range, so the app suggests numbers from your offer, and points you to real salary sources. And finally, it has to work under pressure. If the A.I. service fails, practice switches to an offline manager automatically, at about one cent per reply.
Let's walk through it as a user. I'll load an example: a senior product designer with an eighty-five thousand dollar offer. If she asks for seven percent more, that gap compounds to about two hundred eighty-three thousand dollars over her career. That's more than three years of her salary.
Next, she builds her ask. The app suggests a target and a walk-away from her offer, and shows her counter update live. On the evidence screen, she can jot rough notes, and tap Help me phrase it, which turns them into clear wins, without inventing any numbers.
Here's her ask: a counter of ninety-seven thousand dollars, and a word-for-word script. The highlighted line shows she cares about the relationship. And the What women hear tab prepares her for comments women are more likely to get, like: that feels a little aggressive.
Now the most important part: rehearsing. She's on a call with a hiring manager who's under pressure. First, a nervous reply: sorry, I was just hoping for a bit more? The app underlines the words that weaken her ask, scores her as hesitant, and the manager pushes back, just like a real one might. Right under her message, it suggests a stronger way to say it.
This time, she answers out loud, using the microphone. Her words are transcribed as she speaks. The app checks how she sounded: her pace, filler words, pauses, and whether her voice trails off. Her reply lands in the warm and firm zone, and the manager raises the offer.
When she ends the call, she gets a recap: her scores across the conversation, her best line, and the one habit to work on next time.
Finally, her game plan. A simple cue card for the real conversation, her key numbers, and ready answers if they push back. She can copy it, print it, or email it to herself.
Ask For It turns "I should negotiate" into "I've already practiced this." Because you're allowed to ask.
